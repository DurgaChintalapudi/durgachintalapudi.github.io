Organizational Resilience in Critical Digital Infrastructure

A comparative analysis of the 2024 Change Healthcare cyberattack and CrowdStrike outage, exploring how interdependent technology failures create cascading business risks and what organizations should measure to verify meaningful recovery.

Course: EMGT 7310, Risk Management and Resiliency Analysis
Institution: Texas State University
Project: Module 8 Capstone, Organizational Resiliency Plan
Author: Sridurga Chintalapudi
Classification: Academic coursework analysis, not peer-reviewed research

Executive summary

The Change Healthcare cyberattack and CrowdStrike software-content failure involved different initiating mechanisms, yet both demonstrated how concentrated technology dependencies can disrupt essential services. This case study compares the incidents through an Engineering Management lens: risk identification, preventive control design, business impact analysis, continuity planning, recovery governance, and residual risk. Its proposed organizational controls are analytical recommendations, not claims about verified improvements at either affected company.

1. The two incidents

Change Healthcare: malicious compromise and service disruption

The February 2024 attack on Change Healthcare disrupted healthcare technology and payment-related functions. UnitedHealth Group reported approximately $2.223 billion in direct response costs for 2024 and separately reported $867 million in Optum Insight business disruption impacts. The company also estimated that approximately 190 million individuals were affected [1]. These are reported company-specific figures, not assumptions about a typical claims processor.

CrowdStrike: a defective update and widespread operational failures

On July 19, 2024, a defective CrowdStrike content update triggered widespread crashes on Windows systems. CrowdStrike published a root-cause analysis of the defect and validation process [2]. Microsoft estimated that 8.5 million Windows devices were affected [3]. Unlike the Change Healthcare incident, this failure originated from a software content change rather than an adversarial intrusion.

2. Risk assessment and dependency mapping

The capstone evaluates a hypothetical healthcare claims-processing operator. Its illustrative workflow connects provider intake, identity and access services, transaction validation, databases, payer-routing queues, acknowledgements, and reconciliation. A failure in a shared dependency can interrupt multiple steps simultaneously. The risk categories include cyber, operational change, supplier concentration, financial exposure, privacy, reputation, and continuity.

A business impact analysis (BIA) should establish which functions require priority restoration, the maximum tolerable interruption, and acceptable data-loss limits. Recovery targets and cost assumptions should be validated against workload, contracts, and tested system behavior before informing investment decisions [4,5].

The controls address different failure mechanisms. Neither stronger identity protection nor safer update deployment by itself guarantees business continuity. Management should measure control effectiveness and compare recovery designs against recovery objectives, integrity requirements, financial costs, and residual exposure.

4. Recovery governance and communication

Incident leaders should communicate distinct states: service unavailable; accepting but queued; partially processing; and processing fully reconciled. This distinction prevents an “online” infrastructure status from being mistaken for verified customer-facing recovery.

A business-required recovery time objective is distinct from observed recovery performance. If a recovery test misses the objective, management should record the exception, assign ownership, and determine whether further mitigation or explicit risk acceptance is necessary. Recovery should be verified through end-to-end transactions, not merely server health checks.

5. Sustainability and residual risk

Standby capacity, protected backups, alternate intake mechanisms, and frequent testing create operating, infrastructure, and environmental costs. A defensible organizational plan compares those burdens against continuity requirements using measured workloads rather than unsubstantiated universal ROI claims.

Residual risk remains from shared defects affecting primary and standby systems, compromise of both production and recovery identities, prolonged third-party disruptions, and incomplete reconciliation after restart. These risks require continual review, exercises, and accountable owners.

6. Engineering Management takeaways

  • Analyze dependencies, not just components. A single shared service can carry organization-wide consequences.
  • Separate prevention from continuity. Controls designed for ransomware are not substitutes for safe software-release practices, and vice versa.
  • Use evidence gates. Recovery declarations should depend on tested, auditable service outcomes.
  • Make residual risk explicit. The organization must decide how to handle missed recovery objectives and the cost of additional safeguards.
