When Technology Recovers but Business Doesn't

What the Change Healthcare cyberattack and the CrowdStrike outage reveal about cascading dependencies, measurable control effectiveness, and the difference between restoring systems and restoring essential services.

By Sridurga Chintalapudi
Texas State University · EMGT 7310: Risk Management and Resiliency Analysis
Course project: Module 8 Capstone, Organizational Resiliency Plan
Format: Independent academic case-study analysis, not a peer-reviewed empirical study

The central question: When a major technology dependency fails, how can management know which business functions are affected, choose effective controls, and demonstrate that service has truly recovered?

1. The problem: technical availability is not business continuity

A distributed service can report healthy application instances while essential business work remains incomplete. Transactions may sit in a queue, acknowledgements may be delayed, database replicas may be inconsistent, or external counterparties may still be unreachable. Conversely, a service may be degraded yet still provide enough limited capacity to maintain critical operations.

This creates an engineering management problem rather than merely a troubleshooting task. Senior leaders must decide which business capability to restore first, how much residual risk to accept, whether an alternate processing pathway is adequate, and what evidence justifies communicating that service has resumed.

The capstone's objective was to design a defensible organizational resiliency plan for a hypothetical healthcare claims technology operator using two real 2024 incidents as public evidence. It was not an investigation into either company's undisclosed infrastructure, and it did not run a controlled experiment. Its contribution is a structured, evidence-informed comparison and an actionable approach to measurable risk treatment.

Why this matters: A recovery plan judged only by server uptime could declare success while healthcare providers still cannot reliably submit claims or receive traceable acknowledgements. The measurable unit of recovery must follow the essential business workflow.

2. Why compare Change Healthcare and CrowdStrike?

These incidents were selected deliberately because their initiating causes differ. Change Healthcare illustrates the consequences of malicious compromise of a critical service ecosystem. CrowdStrike illustrates a non-malicious software-content failure propagated through a common technology dependency. Comparing different initiating events helps distinguish cause-specific prevention from shared organizational continuity requirements.

3. What is the analysis method?

The study follows a management-oriented analysis sequence. Each step answers a different question. This is why the article proceeds from a business workflow to dependency failures, controls, recovery decisions, and residual risks.

First, the business impact analysis (BIA) determines which activities are essential and their required recovery objectives. Second, a process map identifies the technical and third-party services necessary to perform those activities. Third, the two incidents show different ways those dependencies can be lost. Fourth, risk controls are specified with accountable owners and observable acceptance tests. Finally, leaders evaluate whether measured recovery meets the business objective and document unresolved risk.

5. The failure mechanisms: different triggers, similar cascading exposure

At Change Healthcare, public testimony describes entry through a remote access portal without multifactor authentication [6]. Identity protection and containment address this initiating scenario, but they do not automatically provide alternate claims-processing capacity. At CrowdStrike, the initiating event was an unintended content update. Strengthening authentication would not have prevented that particular defect; staged release controls and recovery processes are the more relevant safeguards [2].

The managerial insight is that organizations need cause-specific prevention together with cause-independent continuity capabilities. A control portfolio that only strengthens one threat vector may remain exposed to outages originating elsewhere.

6. Which controls solve which problem?

The capstone groups controls into prevention, containment, continuity, and assurance. Each addresses a different mechanism and should have an observable acceptance test. Control effectiveness is not the existence of a policy. A written rollback procedure is not proof that a large fleet can be restored. An untested backup is not demonstrated recovery capacity. A declared RTO is not the same as a measured restoration result. The required evidence must correspond to the failure the control is intended to mitigate.

7. When is a service truly recovered?

The most important recovery distinction is between technical readiness and business readiness. The hypothetical operator should not report full restoration solely because its web endpoint returns success or its servers are running. A recovery decision should inspect actual transaction progress and data consistency.

For example, if identity services and application servers recover but pending transactions still require replay, the service may be technically available yet only partially restored. The business impact analysis defines which functions must resume by what time. The recovery exercise measures what was actually achieved. When the observed recovery exceeds the required objective, management must decide whether to fund stronger controls, adopt a temporary workaround, or document an explicit exception [4,5].

8. Why not simply build maximum redundancy?

Resilience creates trade-offs. Warm standby can improve continuity for an important service but adds recurring infrastructure expense and energy demand. Independent intake protects a subset of business functionality, but creates an eventual replay and reconciliation obligation. More backups can improve recovery options while increasing storage and operational complexity. Active-active architectures can provide rapid failover yet also introduce coordination and data-consistency risks.

The capstone therefore recommends a conditional decision process: conduct the BIA, identify business-required RTO/RPO, test candidate architectures against those requirements, estimate full lifecycle cost and operational burden, and record remaining exposure. It does not assume that one architecture is always optimal, and it does not present hypothetical savings as realized financial outcomes.

Environmental and social consequences also matter. Recovery capacity consumes energy and hardware resources; failed healthcare transaction services may affect providers and customers beyond the direct technology operator. These impacts belong in enterprise risk decisions even when exact costs are difficult to quantify.

9. What did the case study establish, and what remains open?

Main takeaway: Organizational resilience must be evaluated at the level of essential business outcomes. Different technical failures may require different preventive controls, but both demand independently tested continuity, truthful business-state communication, accountable recovery decisions, and explicit residual-risk ownership.

Four lessons for engineering leaders

  1. Different causes, different prevention. Authentication and cyber containment address intrusion risk; release validation, canaries and rollback address defective updates.
  2. Shared dependencies amplify impact. A single identity, update, database, cloud or third-party dependency can interrupt multiple business steps at once.
  3. Recovery claims require business evidence. Healthy servers cannot substitute for successful transactions, complete reconciliation, and verified service capacity.
  4. Risk governance requires tests and owners. A control becomes credible when its acceptance criterion has been independently exercised, with failures escalated rather than hidden.

Scope and limitations

This work is a comparative applied case analysis supported by publicly available evidence and a hypothetical claims-processing model. It does not estimate incident probabilities from a representative dataset, measure the effectiveness of implemented controls in an actual operator, or prove that one recovery architecture has superior cost or performance. Any architecture recommendation remains conditional on measured tests and a validated BIA.
